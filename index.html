Frequency Banks

Total Revenue

Total revenue represents the bank's reported operating revenue which is bifurcated into net interest income and noninterest revenue. As you'll see from the trends below, J.P. Morgan consistently generates the largest amount of revenue while State Street and Bank of New York Mellon are consistently the two smallest. This is primarily due to the nature of each bank's operations: J.P. Morgan is a full-service bank whose operations are tied to the founding of the U.S. economy, whereas State Street and Bank of New York Mellon are primarily custodial banks that serve as intermediaries for more complex financial transactions.

Net Income

Net income is the way U.S. GAAP reports the remainder that is retained and able to distribute to shareholders after key expenses are paid. Typical expenses include operating expenses such as selling, general and administrative as well as additional expenses such as interest, depreciation, and taxes. Diluted earnings per share divides that figure by the total of current and potential shares that would exist if all options and obligations were exercised.

Commentary

The supplied preliminary Q3 net income figures are $15.31 billion at JPMorgan, $3.50 billion at Bank of America, $3.75 billion at Citigroup, $5.95 billion at Wells Fargo, $5.29 billion at Goldman Sachs, $4.91 billion at Morgan Stanley, $0.00 billion at Bank of New York Mellon, and $1.10 billion at State Street. Supplied diluted EPS is $5.57 at JPMorgan, $0.91 at Bank of America, $1.85 at Citigroup, $1.87 at Wells Fargo, $17.01 at Goldman Sachs, $3.03 at Morgan Stanley, $0.00 at Bank of New York Mellon, and $3.69 at State Street. EPS is not directly comparable across banks without considering share counts, capital actions, and differences in business mix.

Note. Diluted EPS reflects share repurchase programs as well as organic earnings growth. When a bank retires its own stock the denominator shrinks, so EPS can rise even in a quarter when net income does not â which is why the two charts above do not always move together.

Consumer Credit Quality

The provision for credit losses is a forward-looking estimate of losses each bank expects to incur in the future. It is booked as a charge against current earnings, so it reduces net income in the quarter it is taken — before any actual loss is realized.

Commentary

The provision for credit losses is a management estimate of expected credit losses and is sensitive to portfolio mix and economic expectations. The supplied preliminary Q3 provisions for credit losses are $2.74 billion at JPMorgan, $1.30 billion at Bank of America, $2.60 billion at Citigroup, $1.00 billion at Wells Fargo, $0.40 billion at Goldman Sachs, $0.15 billion at Morgan Stanley, $0.00 billion at Bank of New York Mellon, and $0.00 billion at State Street. These figures are shown as supplied and should be reconciled to each bankâs release and filed 10-Q; differences in line-item definitions and portfolio mix can limit direct comparisons.

Direction matters as much as level. Changes in provisions should be interpreted alongside portfolio mix, charge-offs, delinquency trends, and managementâs economic outlook rather than as a standalone measure of deteriorating credit quality.

Why these banks are systemically important

The eight institutions tracked hereâJPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, BNY Mellon, and State Streetâare the U.S. banking groups identified as global systemically important banks (G-SIBs). Their importance is not determined by revenue alone. It reflects a combination of scale, interconnectedness with other financial institutions, cross-border activity, complexity, and the difficulty of replacing the critical services they provide.

Channels of systemic importance

Payments, clearing, and custody. These banks support high-volume payments, securities settlement, custody, and asset servicing. Disruption at a major provider can affect many institutions and clients at once.

Credit and capital markets. The large commercial banks provide deposits, lending, liquidity, and credit intermediation, while the investment banks and broker-dealers support underwriting, market-making, and trading. Stress can spread through funding, counterparties, collateral, and asset prices.

Scale and interconnectedness. Revenue is a useful measure of the groupâs scale in this snapshot, but it is not a share of total U.S. banking-sector income. Calculating that share requires a consistent, same-quarter revenue or income denominator for the entire banking sector. Revenue also is not the sole basis for a G-SIB designation.
